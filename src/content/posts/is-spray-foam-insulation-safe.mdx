If you've researched spray foam insulation for more than a few minutes, you've probably run
into unsettling anecdotes — insurance companies scrutinizing it, buyers walking away from home
sales, homeowners paying to have it removed. It's worth addressing this honestly rather than
either dismissing it or letting it scare you off a material that, done right, works well.

## Why Spray Foam Has a Reputation Problem

The concerns are real, and they come from real cases — but the pattern behind them is more
specific than "spray foam is risky." Insurance underwriters have grown cautious around spray
foam installations that were unpermitted, undocumented, or installed by unlicensed applicators,
because those jobs carry a genuinely higher risk of problems down the line. Buyers walking away
from home sales usually trace back to a specific, discoverable issue found during inspection —
not spray foam as a category.

## What Actually Goes Wrong When It's Installed Poorly

Spray foam is a two-component chemical product (commonly called the A side and B side) that has
to be mixed in the correct ratio and applied under the right conditions to cure properly. When
that goes wrong, real problems can follow:

- **Improper mixing ratios** can cause off-gassing — the material doesn't fully cure, and it
  continues releasing chemical odor and potentially harmful compounds into the home long after
  installation.
- **Incorrect application** — too thick a layer applied too quickly can trap heat during curing
  and affect the material's structural integrity.
- **Ventilation and moisture issues** — closed-cell spray foam applied in the wrong location
  (like directly against a roof deck without proper venting design) can trap moisture in a way
  that affects the roof sheathing over time, which is a real warranty and structural concern.
- **Unpermitted or undocumented work** — this is often what actually triggers insurance
  scrutiny or a buyer walking away — not the material itself, but the absence of a paper trail
  showing it was done correctly by a licensed applicator.

## What Proper Installation Looks Like

The fix for essentially all of the above is the same: a licensed, insured, permitted installer
who follows manufacturer specifications, pulls the required permits, and documents the job.
That documentation — what was applied, where, at what thickness, by whom — is exactly what
resolves an insurance underwriter's or a home buyer's concerns, because it demonstrates the
work was done to spec rather than leaving it a guess.

## Spray Foam: Pros and Cons, Fairly Stated

**Pros:** highest R-value per inch of common insulation materials, excellent air-sealing
(fills gaps other materials can't), can add structural rigidity in some closed-cell
applications, long-lasting when properly installed.

**Cons:** significantly more expensive than fiberglass or cellulose, professional installation
only (not a safe DIY project — see our [DIY vs. Professional](/diy-vs-professional-installation)
guide for why), the reputation risk discussed above if installed improperly, more difficult and
costly to remove or modify later than other materials.

## How to Protect Yourself

If you're considering spray foam, the honest advice is straightforward:

1. **Use a licensed, insured installer** — verify this directly, don't just take their word for
   it.
2. **Confirm permits are pulled** for the work, where your jurisdiction requires them.
3. **Ask for documentation** of the specific product, application method, and thickness used.
4. **Keep that documentation** — it protects you at resale and with insurance underwriting down
   the line.

## FAQ

**Is spray foam insulation worth it?** For the right application — air sealing hard-to-reach
gaps, high-performance new construction, spaces where its higher R-value per inch matters —
yes. For a simple attic top-up where fiberglass or cellulose does the job for less, the cost
premium may not be worth it. It depends on your specific project.

**Why do insurance companies not like spray foam insulation?** Most underwriting caution traces
back to undocumented, unpermitted, or improperly installed jobs — not spray foam as a material
category. Properly permitted, professionally installed and documented spray foam doesn't carry
the same red flags.
