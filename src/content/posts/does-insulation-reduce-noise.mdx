If you're dealing with a noisy neighbor, a home office that needs quiet, or a nursery next to a
busy street, you've probably wondered whether adding insulation will help. The honest answer:
somewhat, but insulation alone isn't full soundproofing — here's what actually helps and why.

## How Sound Moves Through Walls

Sound travels two main ways: through the air (airborne sound — voices, music, traffic) and
through solid materials as vibration (structure-borne sound — footsteps, slamming doors). Most
standard walls are actually pretty good at transmitting both, because a typical stud wall with
drywall on both sides acts almost like a drum — vibrations pass through the studs and framing
efficiently.

Insulation helps with the airborne portion by filling the empty cavity inside the wall, which
would otherwise let sound resonate more freely. It does less for structure-borne sound, which
travels through the solid framing itself, bypassing the cavity insulation almost entirely.

## Which Insulation Types Help Most

Not all insulation performs the same acoustically. Roughly, from best to more modest sound
performance for a given thickness:

- **Mineral wool (rock/slag wool)** — denser than fiberglass, generally the best acoustic
  performer among common insulation types, which is why it's frequently specified in
  recording studios and multi-family construction.
- **Cellulose** — denser than standard fiberglass batts, performs reasonably well.
- **Fiberglass** — helps somewhat over an empty cavity, but is the lightest/least dense of the
  common options, so it's the least effective of the three for sound specifically (even though
  it's a fine thermal performer).

Spray foam and rigid foam board are optimized for thermal performance and air sealing, not
acoustic dampening — they're not the go-to choice when sound is the primary goal.

## Insulation Alone Isn't Soundproofing

This is the honest caveat competitors rarely state clearly: filling a wall cavity with denser
insulation measurably reduces sound transmission, but it's one piece of a bigger picture.
Genuine soundproofing typically also involves:

- **Decoupling** — physically separating the two sides of a wall (staggered studs or resilient
  channels) so vibration can't travel directly through a shared frame.
- **Mass** — adding density (like an extra layer of drywall) makes it harder for sound to pass
  through.
- **Sealing gaps** — sound leaks through the smallest air gaps around outlets, baseboards, and
  door frames just as effectively as through an uninsulated cavity.

If noise is a serious problem, insulation upgrades help, but a contractor who understands
acoustic treatment (not just insulation) can advise on whether decoupling or added mass is
worth the extra investment for your situation.

## Best Insulation for Specific Rooms

**Home office:** mineral wool in interior walls, combined with sealing any gaps around the
door, is a reasonable starting point for reducing distraction from adjacent rooms.

**Nursery:** similar approach — mineral wool plus attention to door seals, since a fully quiet
room usually depends more on the door than the walls.

**Shared apartment wall:** insulation upgrades inside a shared wall usually aren't possible for
renters, but mineral wool behind furniture placement and door/window sealing can still help
at the margins.

## FAQ

**Does spray foam block sound?** Not particularly well compared to denser materials like
mineral wool — its strength is thermal performance and air sealing, not acoustic mass.

**What's the best insulation for soundproofing walls?** Mineral wool generally performs best
among standard insulation materials, though true soundproofing usually requires combining it
with decoupling and sealing techniques.
