"Eco-friendly insulation" gets used loosely, so it helps to know what it actually means before
comparing options. Here are the four real criteria, and an honest look at the materials that
tend to qualify.

## What "Eco-Friendly Insulation" Actually Means

Four factors generally determine how sustainable an insulation material is:

- **Recycled content** — how much of the material comes from post-consumer or post-industrial
  recycled sources.
- **VOCs and off-gassing** — whether the material releases volatile organic compounds into your
  home's air, especially right after installation.
- **Embodied energy** — how much energy went into manufacturing and transporting the material in
  the first place.
- **End-of-life recyclability** — what happens to the material when it's eventually removed —
  can it be recycled, or does it go straight to a landfill.

No material scores perfectly on all four, so "eco-friendly" is really a spectrum, not a
pass/fail label.

## Sheep Wool

Sheep wool insulation is a natural fiber with genuinely low embodied energy and no VOC
off-gassing — it's about as close to "raw material" as insulation gets. It also naturally
regulates humidity, which some homeowners value in moisture-prone spaces.

**Pros:** naturally fire-resistant without added chemicals, no off-gassing, biodegradable at
end of life.
**Cons:** significantly more expensive than fiberglass or cellulose, less widely available,
performance can vary by supplier.
**Where it's used:** walls and attics in homes where the cost premium is acceptable for the
material properties.

## Recycled Denim / Cotton Batts

Made primarily from post-industrial recycled cotton and denim fabric scraps, this material
functions similarly to fiberglass batts in terms of installation but starts from recycled
content rather than virgin material.

**Pros:** high recycled content, no itch or skin irritation during installation (a real
practical advantage over fiberglass), decent sound-dampening properties.
**Cons:** costs more than standard fiberglass batts, R-value per inch is comparable to but not
better than fiberglass, availability varies by region.
**Where it's used:** walls, particularly appealing for homeowners prioritizing installer
comfort and recycled content together.

## Cellulose: The "Already Mainstream" Eco Option

Here's a fact many homeowners don't realize: cellulose insulation, made primarily from recycled
newspaper and other paper waste treated with fire retardant, is already one of the more
eco-friendly options on the market — and it's also one of the most common and cost-competitive.
You don't have to seek out a specialty product to get a genuinely high-recycled-content material;
cellulose is a mainstream choice that happens to check the eco-friendly boxes reasonably well.

## What to Ask a Contractor or Manufacturer

If sustainability is a real priority in your decision, it's worth asking directly:

- What percentage of this material is recycled content?
- Does this product carry a third-party certification (like GREENGUARD) for VOC emissions?
- What happens to this material at removal — can it be recycled, or does it go to landfill?

A contractor who can answer these specifically is a good sign; vague answers are worth
following up on.

## FAQ

**Is fiberglass eco-friendly?** Standard fiberglass insulation typically contains some recycled
glass content, but generally less than cellulose, and manufacturing is more energy-intensive.
It's a middle-of-the-pack option on sustainability, not the best or worst.

**Is spray foam eco-friendly?** Spray foam performs very well on energy efficiency (which
reduces long-term energy use), but the manufacturing process and VOC profile during and
shortly after installation are generally less favorable than natural-fiber options. It's a
genuine trade-off depending on which criteria matter most to you.
