Core Insulation Materials

Understanding the fundamentals of the most common insulation types used in residential construction today.

Fiberglass

DIY Friendly

Made from extremely fine glass fibers. Available in batts, rolls, or loose-fill.

Best Uses

  • check_circle Unfinished walls
  • check_circle Floors and ceilings
  • check_circle Standard stud spacing

Cellulose

DIY Friendly

Primarily made from recycled paper products, treated for fire and insect resistance.

Best Uses

  • check_circle Enclosed existing walls
  • check_circle Open new wall cavities
  • check_circle Unfinished attic floors

Spray Foam

Professional Only

Liquid polyurethane that expands into a solid foam, providing excellent air sealing.

Best Uses

  • check_circle Hard-to-reach areas
  • check_circle Around obstructions
  • check_circle Sealing leaks and gaps

Rigid Foam

DIY Friendly

Sturdy panels of polystyrene or polyurethane offering high insulating value per inch.

Best Uses

  • check_circle Unfinished walls (interior)
  • check_circle Unvented low-slope roofs
  • check_circle Basement/foundation walls

Mineral Wool

DIY Friendly

Rock or slag wool that is highly fire-resistant and offers excellent soundproofing.

Best Uses

  • check_circle Fire-rated assemblies
  • check_circle Acoustic damping
  • check_circle Standard stud walls

Specialty & Eco-Friendly Options

Radiant Barrier

Reflects heat rather than absorbing it. Highly effective in hot climates when installed in attics.

Sheep Wool

Natural, sustainable, and capable of absorbing moisture without compromising insulating performance.

Recycled Denim

Treated for fire resistance, offers good sound absorption, and contains no VOCs or formaldehyde.

Is Spray Foam Insulation Safe?

Spray foam insulation is highly effective for air sealing, but it has drawn scrutiny regarding indoor air quality and insurance concerns. The reality is nuanced: installation quality is the primary risk factor.

When mixed and applied correctly by a certified professional, cured spray foam is generally inert and safe. However, improper mixing ratios or insufficient ventilation during application can lead to lingering odors and off-gassing. Furthermore, some mortgage lenders and insurers in certain regions have raised concerns if the foam masks structural timbers, preventing inspection for moisture or termite damage.

Our stance: It is a premium product that requires premium installation. Never attempt a major spray foam job as a DIY project, and always hire vetted contractors who understand local building codes and insurance requirements.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which insulation is best for an attic? expand_more
Blown-in cellulose or loose-fill fiberglass are often considered best for unfinished attic floors because they easily settle around joists and obstructions. For a finished attic or cathedral ceiling, spray foam or rigid foam boards might be necessary to meet R-value requirements in limited space.
Is higher R-value always better? expand_more
While higher R-value means greater resistance to heat flow, it's not the only factor. Air sealing is often just as important as R-value. A poorly sealed home with high R-value insulation will still lose significant energy. The "best" R-value depends on your climate zone and building codes.
Can I mix different types of insulation? expand_more
Yes, combining types is common. For example, "flash and batt" involves applying a thin layer of spray foam for air sealing (flash), followed by fiberglass batts to achieve the desired R-value cost-effectively. However, you must manage vapor barriers carefully to prevent moisture issues.
Does insulation help with soundproofing? expand_more
Yes. Mineral wool is generally considered the best standard insulation for sound attenuation due to its density. Open-cell spray foam and cellulose also offer decent acoustic damping, whereas closed-cell foam and standard fiberglass are less effective for pure soundproofing.
